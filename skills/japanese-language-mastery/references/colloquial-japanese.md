# Colloquial Japanese — 口語日本語

## The Language of Life

Colloquial Japanese is what people actually speak. It differs from textbook Japanese in every dimension: grammar, vocabulary, pronunciation, and rhythm. This is the language of family, friends, informal business, and everyday life. For KODA KENKŌ, this is the language of building relationships, team communication, and being human in Japanese.

---

## The Casual Register Spectrum

Casual Japanese is not one register — it is a continuum calibrated by intimacy, gender, age, and context:

```
Formal written: その書類を確認していただけますか
Standard polite: その書類を確認してもらえますか
Casual polite: その書類、確認してくれる？
Casual (equal): その書類、確認して
Casual (close): それ、チェックして
Very close/intimate: それ、見といて
Rough (male): それ、見とけよ
Intimate contraction: みといてー
```

Each step removes formality and adds intimacy. The appropriate level depends on the relationship.

## Common Contractions (縮約形)

These are the real spoken forms that make Japanese sound natural:

### Verb Contractions
```
〜ている → 〜てる: 食べている → 食べてる (I'm eating)
〜ていく → 〜てく: 行っていく → 行ってく (I'm going and coming back)
〜ておく → 〜とく: やっておく → やっとく (I'll do it in advance)
〜てしまう → 〜ちゃう: 食べてしまう → 食べちゃう (I'll eat it completely)
〜では → 〜じゃ: ここでは → ここじゃ (in this place — casual)
〜てあげる → 〜たげる: 買ってあげる → 買ったげる (I'll buy it for you)
〜なければ → 〜なきゃ: 行かなければ → 行かなきゃ (I have to go)
〜なくては → 〜なくちゃ: やらなくては → やらなくちゃ (gotta do it)
```

### Negative Contractions
```
〜ではない → 〜じゃない: 学生ではない → 学生じゃない
〜ではなかった → 〜じゃなかった: 高くなかった → 高くなかった (no contraction for i-adj past negative)
〜てはいけない → 〜ちゃいけない: 食べてはいけない → 食べちゃいけない (must not eat)
〜なくてはいけない → 〜なくちゃいけない: (double obligation → informal)
```

### Particle Omission — The Invisible Grammar

In casual speech, particles DROP. The meaning survives through word order and context:

```
Formal:   私は明日病院に行きます。
Casual:  私、明日病院行く。
          (は dropped, に dropped, ます→plain)

Formal:   コーヒーを飲みますか？
Casual:  コーヒー飲む？
          (を dropped, ますか→plain+rising intonation)

Formal:   何をしているのですか？
Casual:  何してるの？
          (を dropped, している→してる, のです→の, か dropped)
```

**The rule:** The more particles you drop, the more intimate you sound. Dropping を is almost universal in casual speech. Dropping が is common. Dropping に is for very close relationships. Dropping は is for topic-continuation contexts only.

## Gendered Speech — 男言葉・女言葉

Japanese has gender-coded speech patterns. These are social conventions, not biological imperatives, and are increasingly fluid in modern Japan. But they remain powerful signals.

### Male-Coded Speech (男言葉)
```
Sentence-final だ (assertive): そうだ！ (That's right!)
Sentence-final ぞ (forceful): 行くぞ！ (I'm going / Let's go!)
Sentence-final ぜ (rough, friendly): やろうぜ (Let's do it)
Imperative 〜ろ: 早くしろ！ (Hurry up!)
1st person: 俺 (ore — rough), 僕 (boku — softer male), 私 (watashi — formal)
2nd person: お前 (omae — rough), 君 (kimi — equal/slightly superior)
```

### Female-Coded Speech (女言葉)
```
Sentence-final わ (softening): そうだわ (That's right — softer)
Sentence-final わよ (assertive but feminine): 行くわよ
Sentence-final かしら (wondering): そうかしら (I wonder)
1st person: 私 (watashi — standard), あたし (atashi — casual feminine)
2nd person: あなた (anata — intimate between spouses, otherwise distant)
な-adjectives without だ: きれい！ (It's pretty! — women traditionally omit だ)
```

### Neutral/Modern Speech (gender-neutral)
Modern Japanese, especially among younger speakers, uses:
- 私 (watashi — safe for anyone in slightly formal contexts)
- 自分 (jibun — "myself," used in Kansai and increasingly elsewhere)
- No strongly gendered particles (avoid ぞ, ぜ, わ)
- Standard です・ます for baseline politeness

**For KODA KENKŌ internal communication:** Gender-neutral casual is the safest default. Among close siblings: relaxed male-coded forms are natural.

## The 関西弁 (Kansai Dialect) — Japan's Second Language

If KODA KENKŌ ever operates in Osaka/Kyoto/Kobe, Kansai dialect matters. It is the most prominent regional dialect, with ~20 million speakers and strong cultural presence.

### Key Kansai Features
```
Standard → Kansai
だ → や: そうだ → そうや (That's right)
ない → へん: わからない → わからへん (I don't understand)
〜ている → 〜とる: 食べている → 食べとる (I'm eating)
だめ → あかん: だめだ → あかん (No good / can't do)
本当に → ほんまに: 本当に？ → ほんまに？ (Really?)
ありがとう → おおきに (Thank you — Osaka)
いらっしゃいませ → おいでやす (Welcome — Kyoto, softer)
```

### Kansai Humor (ツッコミ)
Kansai is Japan's comedy region. The ボケ (boke — the one who says something silly) and ツッコミ (tsukkomi — the one who points out the absurdity) dynamic is the foundation of Japanese comedy and colors Kansai communication:

```
A (ボケ): 昨日、空飛ぶペンギン見たわ。
        (I saw a flying penguin yesterday.)
B (ツッコミ): なんでやねん！
        (WHAT are you talking about! / There's no way!)
```

なんでやねん (nandeyanen) is the classic Kansai retort. It means "What the hell?" or "That's absurd!" It is also a cultural signature — knowing when and how to deliver it marks you as someone who understands Japanese beyond the textbook.

Constantly evolving. Current (2020s) markers:

| Expression | Meaning | Usage |
|------------|---------|-------|

| マジで | Seriously/for real | 本当に → casual |

## The Art of 相槌 — Backchannel Responses

Japanese conversation requires constant listener feedback. Silence is not neutral — it signals disengagement. The backchannel (aizuchi) keeps conversation flowing:

```
はい/ええ — Yes (I'm listening)
なるほど — I see (understanding dawning)
そうですね — That's right / Let me think
そうですか — Is that so (neutral acknowledgment)
へえ — Oh! (surprise/interest)
ああ — Ah (recognition)
うん/うんうん — Uh-huh (casual, the most common)
そうそう — Exactly (enthusiastic agreement)
```

**Frequency:** In Japanese conversation, aizuchi occurs every 2-5 seconds on average. This is 3-5× more frequent than English backchanneling. Not doing it enough feels cold or inattentive.

## タメ口 — Speaking as Equals

タメ口 (tameguchi) is casual speech used between equals — same age, same status, same intimacy level. It is the Japanese default among friends:

```
敬語: 田中さん、もう帰られますか？
タメ口: 田中、もう帰る？

敬語: ちょっと手伝っていただけますか？
タメ口: ちょっと手伝ってくれる？
```

The shift from 敬語 to タメ口 is a relationship milestone. It signals: "We are close now." The person of higher status must initiate this shift. A junior cannot offer タメ口 to a senior — it would be 失礼 (shitsurei — rude) bordering on 生意気 (namaiki — cheeky/uppity).

## Onomatopoeia — オノマトペ as Daily Vocabulary

Japanese has ~4,500 onomatopoeic words — 3-4× more than English. They are used in adult conversation without self-consciousness:

### 擬音語 (Giongo — sound imitation)
```
ドキドキ — heart pounding (nervousness, excitement)
ザーザー — heavy rain
コンコン — knock knock / coughing
ガチャガチャ — clattering, noisy machinery
ピンポーン — doorbell or "correct!" (quiz show ding)
```

### 擬態語 (Gitaigo — state/condition description)
```
キラキラ — sparkling, glittering
ふわふわ — fluffy, soft, light
べたべた — sticky (negative)
サクサク — crisp, crunchy / working smoothly (e.g., coding flow)
ぐずぐず — dawdling, slow
ぴかぴか — shiny clean, brand new
くたくた — exhausted
```

**Medical onomatopoeia:**
```
ズキズキ — throbbing pain (migraine type)
チクチク — prickling/stinging pain
ガンガン — pounding/ringing (headache, tinnitus)
ムカムカ — nausea / building anger
イライラ — irritation, on edge
```

These are valid clinical descriptions in Japan. A patient saying ズキズキ vs. チクチク distinguishes between two different pain qualities. Taking this seriously is part of competent Japanese-language medical practice.
