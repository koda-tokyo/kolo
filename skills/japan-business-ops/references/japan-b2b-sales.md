## 1. Sales Cycle Overview

Japanese enterprise sales cycles are significantly longer than Western equivalents. Expect:

| Phase | Duration | Key Activity |
|-------|----------|-------------|
| Initial Contact | 1-3 months | Introduction via trusted intermediary; first meeting is exploratory, no pitch |
| Relationship Building | 2-6 months | Multiple non-commercial meetings; establish trust and mutual understanding |
| Needs Discovery | 1-3 months | Joint problem definition; customer expects vendor to deeply understand their context |
| Formal Evaluation | 2-6 months | Technical validation, reference checks, site visits to existing customers |
| Proposal & Negotiation | 1-4 months | Multiple proposal iterations; pricing discussions are late-stage |
| Decision & Contract | 1-3 months | Ringi-sho circulation; legal/compliance review |
| Total Typical | 8-24 months | From first contact to signed contract |

**Key insight:** Attempting to compress or skip phases damages trust irreparably.

## 2. Decision-Making Structure

### Nemawashi (根回し)
Pre-meeting consensus building. Before any formal decision meeting:
- Meet individually with each stakeholder
- Present the proposal and gather concerns privately
- Revise proposal to address each concern
- Only call the formal meeting when consensus is already assured
- The formal meeting (会議) ratifies what has already been agreed

**Failure mode:** Presenting at a formal meeting without nemawashi → polite silence → no decision → "we'll consider it" (permanent limbo).

### Ringi-sho (稟議書)
The formal decision document that circulates through the organization:
- Document passes sequentially through hierarchy (bottom → top)
- Each person affixes their hanko (seal) to approve
- Anyone in the chain can block or delay
- Ringi is a consensus mechanism, not an approval workflow
- A single objection typically requires rework, not override

**Tactical implications:**
- Know whose hanko is needed (map the ringi chain early)
- Provide supporting materials each approver needs
- Prepare Q&A documents anticipating likely concerns
- The ringi process cannot be rushed; plan for 4-8 weeks

### Decision-Making Body Roles

| Role | Japanese | Function |
|------|----------|----------|
| Business Owner | 担当者 | Day-to-day contact, evaluates product, writes ringi draft |
| Section Chief | 課長 (Kachō) | First management approval; operational decision authority |
| Department Head | 部長 (Buchō) | Strategic approval; budget authority; key influencer |
| Executive Officer | 役員 (Yakuin) | High-value deals; may attend closing meetings ceremonially |
| CEO/President | 社長 (Shachō) | Final ringi approval; relationship-level engagement only |

## 3. Relationship Building & Trust

**Trust before transaction** (取引前の信頼構築) is the cardinal rule:
- Japanese enterprises buy from people they trust, not from companies
- No amount of product superiority replaces relationship deficit
- Relationships are built person-to-person, not organization-to-organization
- Changing your contact person resets trust to near zero

**Trust-building activities:**
- Regular non-sales visits (情報交換, "information exchange")
- Introduction of senior executives (役員挨拶)
- After-hours dining (懇親会, 飲み会) — critical for candid communication
- Small gifts from home country (お土産) — thoughtful, not expensive
- New Year greetings (年賀状) and seasonal correspondence (暑中見舞い)
- Remembering personal details (children, hobbies, career history)

**Measuring relationship depth:**
1. Formal meetings only → Early stage
2. Invited to lunch → Progressing
3. Invited to evening drinking → Relationship established
4. Introduced to their superiors → Trust earned
5. Asked for informal advice → Deep trust

## 4. Pricing & Negotiation

### Pricing Culture
- Never lead with price; price discussion is late-stage only
- Initial pricing sets permanent anchor — discounting damages long-term positioning
- Japanese buyers expect detailed cost breakdowns (見積書)
- Annual price negotiations are the default (not per-deal)
- Competitor price comparisons are commonly used as leverage

### Negotiation Protocol
- Negotiations are slow and multi-round
- Direct confrontation is relationship-damaging
- Use "difficult" (難しい) rather than "impossible" (不可能)
- Silence is a negotiation tool — don't fill it
- Concessions must appear earned, never easy
- Final price agreement often happens at 部長 level or above
- Written quotation (見積書) is binding — never quote loosely

### Pricing Models That Work
| Model | Acceptability | Notes |
|-------|--------------|-------|
| Annual license | Standard | Predictable budgeting; preferred |
| Monthly SaaS | Growing | Still less common; procurement unfamiliar |
| Per-user | Acceptable | Clear cost-per-person logic |
| Usage-based | Difficult | Budget cannot be predicted; pass |
| Revenue share | Rare | Only in joint venture contexts |

## 5. After-Sales Service

Japanese after-sales expectations are the highest in the world:
- 24/7 support is table stakes, not premium
- Response time expectations: minutes, not hours
- On-site presence expected for critical issues
- Proactive status reporting (no news is NOT good news)
- Root cause analysis reports (原因分析報告書) expected for any incident
- Support quality defines reputation more than product quality

**Support structure expectations:**
- Japanese-speaking support (not just English)
- Japan-time-zone availability
- Dedicated support contact (not anonymous ticket system)
- Regular on-site visits (定例訪問) even when nothing is wrong
- Escalation path transparency

## 6. Key Decision-Maker Roles

### Identifying the Real Decision Maker
- The person who speaks most in meetings is rarely the decision maker
- The most senior person in the room may be ceremonial
- The person who asks the most detailed questions is often the key influencer
- The kachō (課長) is typically the operational decision maker
- The person writing the ringi-sho controls the narrative

### Champions (推進者)
Every successful Japanese enterprise deal has an internal champion (推進者):
- This person invested personal capital in bringing you in
- They need ammunition to advocate internally
- Provide them materials in Japanese they can circulate
- Never embarrass them or bypass them
- Their career risk is real — failure reflects on them
- Protect and support them — they are your most valuable asset
